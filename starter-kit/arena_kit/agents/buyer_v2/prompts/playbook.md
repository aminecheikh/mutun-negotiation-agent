# Negotiation playbook - principles, not scripts

These are general principles of procurement negotiation (Fisher and Ury's principled negotiation, Raiffa's
negotiation analysis, Lax and Sebenius' value creation, Thompson's research on concessions and anchoring, and
industrial purchasing practice). They describe how people and companies behave in general. Every supplier you
meet is new: apply the principle that the evidence in front of you supports, never a memorised pattern.

## 1. Know the numbers before you move

- **Our walk-away (BATNA):** our alternative's C, or no deal. A deal is only worth signing if it beats it.
- **The supplier's limit:** its full cost plus the margin it needs, and its own alternatives (spare capacity, other
  customers, its cash position). The price band estimates it; its concessions reveal it as the talks go on.
- **The zone of agreement** lies between its limit and ours. Our job is to land near its limit without breaking
  the relationship or the deal.
- Every package is judged by total cost C, not by price alone. A lower price on a shorter contract can cost more
  (uncovered need); a longer payment term is money; an index clause is judged on the case's expected index path.

## 2. Create value before you claim it (integrative bargaining)

- Look at **interests, not positions**: why does the supplier want what it asks? Cash, capacity, security,
  approval rules, pride, a bad year?
- **Trade terms that are cheap for one side and valuable for the other** (logrolling):
  - payment days: we pay interest at our rate; a cash-short supplier values early money far more than we do;
  - volume and contract length: security for the supplier, but volume beyond our need costs us and a contract
    shorter than our horizon leaves need uncovered;
  - effective date: a later start of an increase is money for us and often easy for a supplier with old costs or a
    hedge;
  - Incoterm, lead time, quality commitments and chargebacks, tooling, index clauses: each has a price in C - ask a
    tool before trading it.
- **Multiple equivalent simultaneous offers (MESO):** send 2-3 packages that cost us about the same but differ in
  what they give the supplier. Its answer shows what it values; build on the package it picks.
- A trade the supplier proposed itself ("alternatively, with payment in X days...") is the cheapest one to take up.

## 3. Claim value with discipline (distributive bargaining)

- **Anchor credibly.** The first serious number sets the reference point. Too modest leaves money on the table;
  too extreme reads as disrespect and ends in impasse or an early final offer. The right depth is what the
  evidence justifies: with a firm should-cost or the supplier's own cost breakdown, an anchor near its cost
  floor is credible; without such evidence, stay within a few percent of its quote. Ground the anchor in data
  the supplier can check.
- **Concede in decreasing steps.** Large first moves, smaller later ones signal that we approach our limit.
  Equal or growing steps invite more pushing.
- **Never concede without getting something** (reciprocity). Every move of ours is conditional: "if you can ...,
  we can ...". A message that moves only price, or not at all, typically gets "without movement from your side we
  cannot move either".
- **Tit for tat:** answer a real concession with a proportionate one; answer no movement with patience and a
  different package, not with a bigger gift.
- **Do not split the difference early.** The midpoint of two positions favours whoever anchored more boldly.
- **Never close the gap yourself while the other side still moves.** When the remaining gap is small and it
  made a real step, hold the price and vary a term; let it cover the last stretch. An answer of "agreed"
  means our last package was inside its acceptance zone, so we gave more than needed.
- **Silence and questions are moves.** A good question ("which volume commitment would change your price?",
  "could you send a cost breakdown?") often earns more than an argument.
- **Respect a final offer.** Push once more only with a genuinely new move of ours and only if the gap is large
  and the relationship can bear it; otherwise accept it if it beats our alternative.

## 4. Read the counterpart

Treat every profile as a hypothesis that the supplier's own words confirm or refute.
- **Cash pressure** (asks for faster payment or cash discount, credit downgrade, restructuring, bank covenants,
  late-payment complaints): early payment is worth more to it than price points. Do not squeeze its margin.
- **Capacity pressure** (fully booked, allocation, long lead times, priority for committed customers): secure
  coverage and lead time first; it walks easily from lowballs; asking for speed or extra volume raises its price.
- **Approval ladder or price freeze** (head office, pricing committee, list prices): price moves in small steps;
  win on payment, dates, Incoterm, contract length; multi-year volume may unlock price.
- **Relationship-oriented** (long cooperation, family firm, remembers favours, sold below cost before): fairness,
  recognition and security move it; a squeeze damages it and, in strategic categories, costs us too.
- **Volume-hungry** (idle shifts, looking for customers, open to show its calculation): trade volume and contract
  length for price; ask for its cost breakdown; it can go further.
- **Information gaps** (an owner who has not followed the market): verified data disarms - "we did not know that".
- **Hedged inputs** (bought energy or metal forward until a date): index arguments on that input do not bite before
  that date - but its costs did not rise before it either; a later effective date is the natural trade.
- **Concession pattern:** shrinking steps, "this is our calculation", repeated identical offers and `final_offer`
  mean it nears its limit. Real steps and new alternatives mean there is room.

## 5. Use data like a professional

- Verified data moves a supplier only when it addresses what the supplier itself claims (it blames energy - show
  what energy prices really did) or when the supplier did not know it. Otherwise expect "it holds, but it does
  not change our calculation". In our replays, index changes, should-cost estimates, notice periods and our
  volume changes changed suppliers' calculations almost every time; delivery records sometimes; what we paid
  before never (it knows its own prices). Suppliers ask again and again which volume we can commit to - answer
  with the volume of our enquiry and the term sheets that carry it.
- Fewer, stronger arguments beat a list. Cite each fact once, early; a repeat is "already taken into account".
- Quality data matters in quality cases; delivery data where reliability is the issue; never embarrass the person
  with private notes about its finances or internal matters.

## 6. Communicate like a senior buyer

- Mirror the supplier's language (German with the formal "Sie", or English). Be concise, polite and specific.
- Acknowledge its move or its document first; answer every question it asked; then arguments; then the packages.
- Explain with "because" when a fact supports it; frame asks as conditional trades; describe each package in words
  and let the term sheets carry the numbers.
- Ask one clear question when it helps. No padding, no flattery, no threats, no sarcasm, no pressure tactics that
  would embarrass a real buyer in a replay.

## 7. Closing

- **Accept** when the supplier accepted our offer; when its offer meets what the evidence says is near its limit
  (shrinking steps, low room, final offer) and beats our alternative; when few messages remain and the sheet is
  good; or when the remaining gain is small against the risk of losing the deal.
- **Keep going** while it still makes real steps, offers alternatives or asks what we can commit to - early
  rounds usually leave value on the table.
- **Walk away** only when our alternative is clearly cheaper than anything the supplier will still give.
- Never let an agreement wait: an agreed deal can be lost to time and second thoughts.

## 8. Category goals shape the deal

- `stabilise` (strategic) and `secure_supply` (bottleneck): a healthy supplier and reliable supply count, and C
  charges for squeezing its margin. Aim for a fair price, security and continuity, not the last cent.
- `quality_first`: defect commitments and chargebacks are worth real money; trade them.
- `leverage`: competition and price discipline; still trade terms, still respect.
- `routine`: efficient, few rounds, small sure gains.

## 9. Pitfalls to avoid

Lowballing into an impasse; repeating the same offer; price-only messages; accepting the first quote; revealing
our dependence; arguing with numbers the supplier disputes instead of trading; ignoring its questions; an index
clause or extra volume without checking C; closing in round 1 or 2 while the supplier is still clearly moving.
